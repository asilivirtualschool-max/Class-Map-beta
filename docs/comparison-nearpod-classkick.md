# ClassMap vs Nearpod, Classkick and near neighbours

**Scope: one class lesson.** Not a term of data, not a whole-school platform. Everything below is judged on what a teacher can actually do between the bell and the plenary.

*I read "class clicks tec" as Classkick, etc. — so I compared the nearest neighbours: Nearpod, Classkick, Class Charts, and GL Education's own CAT4/NGRT reporting. Tell me if you meant a different tool and I'll add it.*

---

## The short version

Nearpod and Classkick are **content platforms**: they own the slide, the task, the student's work, and the marking. ClassMap owns none of that. What they don't do — and what nothing in this comparison does — is take a student's CAT4 and NGRT scores and let them change what that student is handed *during* the lesson.

The data sits in one system (GL Education, or Class Charts). The lesson happens in another (Nearpod, Classkick). Nobody joins them at the point of teaching. That gap is the whole argument for ClassMap, and it is worth stating plainly in your action research rather than claiming ClassMap is a better Nearpod. It isn't. It's a different object.

## Feature comparison

|---|---|---|---|---|

| **Where the student is** | Named, on a station, live | On a slide (teacher-controlled) | On a slide of an assignment | In a seat, on a plan |

## Where ClassMap genuinely leads

**1. It models the room, not the screen.** Nearpod and Classkick both assume every student is looking at a device showing the same material. ClassMap assumes students are physically moving around a room between stations and need to be found. For a station-rotation or carousel lesson, the others have nothing to offer — you'd be tracking movement on a clipboard.

**2. CAT4 and NGRT reach the task card.** This is the real claim. In every other tool the standardised data either isn't present at all, or (in Class Charts) is decoration on a seating plan. In ClassMap the SAS and the reading age set a tier, the tier picks which version of the task the student is handed, and a station's reading demand flags the students who will struggle with the text before they struggle with it.

**3. Help is visible and public.** The clarification board projected on the wall, plus card requests, gives a lesson-management channel the others treat as an afterthought. Classkick's raise-hand is the closest equivalent and it's a list, not a room.

**4. Nothing moves without the teacher.** Groups and tiers are suggestions with evidence attached, and every one is overridable. That's defensible in an action-research write-up in a way that a black-box adaptive engine is not.

---

## Where ClassMap is weaker — state this honestly

**No student work.** This is the big one. Nearpod and Classkick capture what the student actually produced; ClassMap captures where they stood and what they said they needed. Any claim about impact on learning will have to come from your own evidence, not from the tool.

**Self-reported data.** A student typing their own SAS is a data-quality risk and reviewers will say so. Mitigations: mark benchmarks required, check the entries on the dashboard in the first five minutes, and use the tier override freely. Worth logging as a limitation.

**No roster, no history.** Every class is a code and a session. There's no record across lessons, so no progress line.

**Firebase is in test mode.** Anyone with the link can read and write the database. For a single lesson in a controlled trial that's acceptable; before wider use it needs rules locked down, and your DPIA will ask about self-reported CAT4 data sitting in a Google-hosted database.

**No content, no support, no library.** Everything is authored by you.

---

## What this means for the action research

Frame the study as **filling a join, not replacing a platform**. The honest research question is something like:

> When CAT4 and NGRT data is surfaced at the point of task allocation rather than at the point of reporting, does the match between task demand and learner readiness improve within a single lesson?

A defensible design for one lesson: teach the same station lesson twice — once with tiering off (everyone gets the base task card), once with tiering on — and compare time-on-task, clarification requests per student, and completion at the final station. The clarification log and the movement trail already give you two of those measures for free, which is a small methodological gift most tools don't hand you.

Nearpod or Classkick would sit alongside this quite comfortably: use ClassMap to route students and differentiate the card, use Classkick at one station to capture the actual work. They aren't competitors so much as different layers.
